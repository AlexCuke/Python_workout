The door was set deep into a stone wall, black with age and sealed shut for as long as anyone could remember. 
No one ever went in, and no one ever came out. Children would dare each other to touch its cold, iron handle,
 but they would always run away giggling before they could turn it. For Arthur, it was simply a part of the 
landscape, as unchanging as the old church tower.One Tuesday morning, however, something was different. The 
door was slightly ajar. A sliver of darkness showed between the door and its frame. Arthur stopped. His heart, 
usually steady, gave a small, unexpected thump. He felt a strange pull, an urge to walk over and push it open, 
a feeling he had not experienced since he was a boy.He crossed the lane, his footsteps loud in the quiet morning. 
As he grew closer, he noticed the air around the door felt cooler, and the usual sounds of the town—a distant 
dog barking, the hum of a car—seemed to fade away. He reached out a hand, his fingers trembling slightly, and 
touched the worn wood. It was smooth, like glass.With a gentle push, the door swung inward without a sound. 
It did not open to a garden, a courtyard, or even a derelict building, as he had always imagined. Instead, 
a wide, grassy field stretched out before him under a pale sky. In the distance, he could see a figure sitting 
on a wooden bench under a large, solitary tree. The figure turned, and even from far away, Arthur recognized 
the shape of his wife, Mary, who had passed away ten years ago. She smiled and waved, just as she used to do 
when he came home from work.Arthur stood on the threshold, one foot in the dusty lane of Bletchley and one 
foot on the soft grass of the field. The air behind him smelled of exhaust fumes and rain. The air in front 
of him smelled of fresh earth and wildflowers. He had a choice to make.
